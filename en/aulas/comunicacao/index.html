Lesson 5 · 16 min

Communication is everything

High technical skill with weak communication delivers less than average technical skill with good communication. The client speaks in adjectives ("a lot," "slow," "confusing"); the people who decide read numbers. This lesson shows you how to turn the vague into a number, talk about budget without awkwardness, and deliver everything on one page.

Start the lesson

In this lesson you will

  1. 1Go from the request down to the cause with five whys
  2. 2Turn each of the client's adjectives into a number
  3. 3Ask about budget without sizing up anyone's wallet
  4. 4Write the one-page report for an executive

Goal: turn a vague request into a requirement with a number, talk about budget without awkwardness, and write the one-page report.

01 · From vague to one page

Each step turns what the client said into something the board approves.

Click each block in the diagram to see what it does.

The success question helps at every step: "Six months from now the project worked; what exactly changed in the business?"

02 · Vague becomes a number

What the client says and what you give back.

1. "A lot of tickets"

Ask for the quantity and the peak.

You replyHow many per day? And what's the peak of the week?

2. "The process is slow"

Ask for the unit of time.

You replySlow by how many hours: per day, per week, per month?

3. "We make a lot of mistakes"

An error with no cost doesn't go in the proposal.

You replyHow many errors per hundred handoffs, and what does each one cost?

4. Paraphrase

Sum it up in one sentence and ask if that's right.

When the summary is wrong, the client corrects it with details they would never give away for free.

You replySo the real problem is the approval queue, and the form just exposes that queue. Is that right?

03 · In the meeting

Four moves and the right close.

1. Opening

Say who you are and tie your goal to theirs. It's not a zero-sum game.

PhraseMy goal is for this project to lower your cost per ticket. If it does, you'll call me again.

2. Hypothesis

Show up with 2 or 3 hypotheses and ask for corrections. It's easier to edit a finished text than to face a blank page.

PhraseFrom my research, these are the three main points. Correct me if I'm wrong.

3. Budget

"What's your budget?" sounds like "can you afford it?".

Don't apologize before talking about price, and don't push the topic off to an email later.

PhraseIs there any budget already set aside for AI initiatives? It helps me work backward from your expectations.

4. Close

Never end with "I'll send you a proposal." Book the next meeting before this one is over.

A slow proposal is read as lack of priority.

04 · Templates to copy

The report is the piece your contact takes to the board without you in the room.

One-page report for executives
<COMPANY> · <area> · <date>
To: <title of the decision-maker>

PROBLEM (2 lines)
<the pain in the client's own words and why it costs money>

NUMBER
Today: <metric> = <value>, measured by <how>.
Estimated cost: <value per month> (assumption: <which>).

RECOMMENDED OPTION
<what to do, in 3 lines, with deadline and owner>
Cheaper alternative: <which one and what is lost>

RISK
<what could go wrong and how we'll know early>
Not included: <...>

DECISION REQUESTED
<yes/no for the pilot> by <date>.
Budget and constraint questions
BUDGET
- Is there any budget already set aside for AI initiatives at the company?
- It helps me work backward from your expectations.

CONSTRAINTS (they weigh more on price than scope)
- Which systems does this process go through, from start to finish?
- Who grants access to them, and how long does that usually take?
- Is there any data here that can't leave the company?

SUCCESS
- Picture six months from now. The project was a total success.
  What exactly changed in the business?
Prompt · reconnaissance before the meeting
Research this company and give me back a meeting briefing.
I don't want flashy ideas: I want the most practical ones to implement.

Company: <public name and sector>
Area I'll be talking to: <area or title>
Approximate size: <number of employees>

Return in this order:
1. Business model and where the revenue comes from, in 5 lines.
2. How the area probably operates today: steps and tools.
3. The 5 most practical scenarios, ordered by ease of implementation.
4. For each scenario, the business justification in one sentence.
5. The 3 questions I'll ask to confirm or knock down each hypothesis.
Explicitly mark what is public fact and what is your own assumption.

05 · Practice

Use the case from your proposal in lesson 3.

1 · Three adjectives into numbers

15 min

List 3 vague phrases your client (or the clinic from lesson 2) said, and write the question that turns each one into a number.

Done when: the 3 phrases each have a question next to them, and each question asks for quantity, time or cost.

2 · One-page report

40 min

Fill in the executive report template. Read it out loud: you should be able to decide in two minutes.

Done when: the report fits on one page and has the problem, a number with how it's measured, a recommended option, the risk and the decision requested with a date.

07 · Summary

Communication multipliesFraming the problem in the client's own words wins half the meeting.
Vague becomes a numberTurn every adjective into a quantity, a time, or a cost, right in front of the client.
Budget without awkwardnessAsk about the company's decision, not about the pocket of the person on the call.
One pageProblem, number, recommendation, and risk. And the next meeting already scheduled.
